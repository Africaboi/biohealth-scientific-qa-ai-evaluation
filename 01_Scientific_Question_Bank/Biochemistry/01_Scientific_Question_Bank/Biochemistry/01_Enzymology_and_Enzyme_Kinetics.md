## Question 1: Michaelis-Menten Kinetics

An enzyme-catalysed reaction follows Michaelis-Menten kinetics. At a substrate concentration equal to the Michaelis constant (Km), what proportion of the maximum reaction velocity (Vmax) is achieved?

A. 25%  
B. 50%  
C. 75%  
D. 100%

**Correct Answer: B. 50%**

### Scientific Explanation

The Michaelis-Menten equation is:

v = (Vmax × [S]) / (Km + [S])

Where:

- v = initial reaction velocity
- Vmax = maximum reaction velocity
- [S] = substrate concentration
- Km = Michaelis constant

When [S] = Km:

v = (Vmax × Km) / (Km + Km)

Therefore:

v = Vmax / 2

Thus, when substrate concentration equals Km, the reaction velocity is half of Vmax.

### Evaluation of Alternative Answers

- A. 25%: Incorrect. This is not the velocity predicted when substrate concentration equals Km.
- C. 75%: Incorrect. This would require a substrate concentration greater than Km.
- D. 100%: Incorrect. Vmax is approached at very high substrate concentrations but is not reached at [S] = Km.

## Question 2: Competitive Enzyme Inhibition

A researcher investigates the effect of a reversible competitive inhibitor on an enzyme. Which kinetic change is expected?

A. Decreased Vmax and unchanged Km  
B. Increased apparent Km and unchanged Vmax  
C. Decreased Km and decreased Vmax  
D. Increased Vmax and increased Km

**Correct Answer: B. Increased apparent Km and unchanged Vmax**

### Scientific Explanation

A competitive inhibitor competes with the substrate for binding to the enzyme's active site.

Increasing the substrate concentration can overcome competitive inhibition because substrate molecules can outcompete the inhibitor for access to the active site.

Consequently:

- Vmax remains unchanged because sufficiently high substrate concentrations can restore the maximum reaction velocity.
- Apparent Km increases because a higher substrate concentration is required to achieve half of Vmax.

### Evaluation of Alternative Answers

- A. Incorrect. This describes the characteristic effect of pure noncompetitive inhibition on Vmax and Km.
- C. Incorrect. This is not the expected pattern for competitive inhibition.
- D. Incorrect. Competitive inhibition does not increase Vmax.

## Question 3: Pure Noncompetitive Inhibition

An enzyme is exposed to a pure noncompetitive inhibitor. Which combination of kinetic effects is expected?

A. Increased Km and unchanged Vmax  
B. Decreased Km and decreased Vmax  
C. Unchanged Km and decreased Vmax  
D. Unchanged Km and increased Vmax

**Correct Answer: C. Unchanged Km and decreased Vmax**

### Scientific Explanation

A pure noncompetitive inhibitor binds to both the free enzyme and the enzyme-substrate complex with equal affinity.

This reduces the concentration of catalytically functional enzyme without changing the enzyme's apparent affinity for the substrate.

Therefore:

- Vmax decreases because the effective concentration of active enzyme is reduced.
- Km remains unchanged because substrate binding affinity is not altered under the pure noncompetitive inhibition model.

### Evaluation of Alternative Answers

- A. Incorrect. This is the typical kinetic pattern of competitive inhibition.
- B. Incorrect. This is not the characteristic pattern of pure noncompetitive inhibition.
- D. Incorrect. Pure noncompetitive inhibition reduces rather than increases Vmax.

## Question 4: Uncompetitive Enzyme Inhibition

Which statement correctly describes uncompetitive inhibition?

A. The inhibitor binds only to the free enzyme.  
B. The inhibitor binds only to the enzyme-substrate complex.  
C. The inhibitor competes directly with the substrate for the active site.  
D. The inhibitor permanently destroys the enzyme.

**Correct Answer: B. The inhibitor binds only to the enzyme-substrate complex.**

### Scientific Explanation

Uncompetitive inhibitors bind exclusively to the enzyme-substrate (ES) complex, forming an inactive enzyme-substrate-inhibitor complex.

This type of inhibition produces two characteristic kinetic effects:

- Decreased apparent Vmax.
- Decreased apparent Km.

Both parameters decrease by the same factor in the classical uncompetitive inhibition model.

The reduction in apparent Km occurs because inhibitor binding stabilises the enzyme-substrate complex, favouring substrate association with the enzyme.

### Evaluation of Alternative Answers

- A. Incorrect. Binding exclusively to free enzyme is associated with competitive inhibition.
- C. Incorrect. Direct competition for the active site characterises competitive inhibition.
- D. Incorrect. Uncompetitive inhibition is generally reversible and does not require permanent enzyme destruction.

## Question 5: Enzyme Specificity

Which feature most directly explains the high substrate specificity of many enzymes?

A. Their ability to increase reaction temperature  
B. The structural and chemical complementarity of the active site to the substrate and reaction transition state  
C. Their ability to change the equilibrium constant of a reaction  
D. Their ability to increase the free-energy difference between reactants and products

**Correct Answer: B. The structural and chemical complementarity of the active site to the substrate and reaction transition state**

### Scientific Explanation

Enzyme specificity is largely determined by the structural arrangement and chemical properties of amino acid residues within the active site.

These features enable selective molecular recognition through interactions such as:

- Hydrogen bonding
- Ionic interactions
- Hydrophobic interactions
- Van der Waals interactions

Enzymes achieve catalytic acceleration primarily by stabilising the transition state and lowering the activation energy of a reaction.

They do not alter the overall equilibrium constant or the free-energy difference between reactants and products.

### Evaluation of Alternative Answers

- A. Incorrect. Enzymes do not generally increase reaction temperature.
- C. Incorrect. Enzymes accelerate the attainment of equilibrium without changing the equilibrium constant.
- D. Incorrect. Enzymes lower activation energy rather than changing the overall free-energy difference between reactants and products.

## Question 6: Allosteric Enzyme Regulation

An enzyme displays a sigmoidal relationship between reaction velocity and substrate concentration. What does this behaviour most commonly indicate?

A. Irreversible enzyme inhibition  
B. Cooperative substrate binding  
C. Complete enzyme denaturation  
D. Absence of substrate binding sites

**Correct Answer: B. Cooperative substrate binding**

### Scientific Explanation

Sigmoidal enzyme kinetics are commonly associated with cooperative interactions between substrate-binding sites in allosteric enzymes.

In positive cooperativity, substrate binding at one site increases the likelihood of substrate binding at other sites.

This produces a sigmoidal velocity-versus-substrate concentration curve rather than the typical hyperbolic curve associated with simple Michaelis-Menten kinetics.

The Hill coefficient is commonly used to describe the degree of cooperativity:

- nH greater than 1: positive cooperativity.
- nH equal to 1: no apparent cooperativity.
- nH less than 1: negative cooperativity.

The Hill coefficient is not necessarily identical to the number of substrate-binding sites.

### Evaluation of Alternative Answers

- A. Incorrect. Irreversible inhibition does not specifically explain sigmoidal substrate-response behaviour.
- C. Incorrect. Denaturation generally compromises enzyme structure and function.
- D. Incorrect. Sigmoidal kinetics are associated with substrate binding and cooperative regulation.

## Question 7: Effect of Temperature on Enzyme Activity

Why does the catalytic activity of many enzymes decrease substantially above their optimal temperature?

A. Substrate concentration always decreases at higher temperatures.  
B. High temperatures can disrupt interactions responsible for maintaining the enzyme's functional three-dimensional structure.  
C. High temperatures permanently increase enzyme-substrate affinity.  
D. Enzymes stop participating in chemical reactions at all temperatures above 25°C.

**Correct Answer: B. High temperatures can disrupt interactions responsible for maintaining the enzyme's functional three-dimensional structure.**

### Scientific Explanation

Increasing temperature generally increases molecular kinetic energy and can initially accelerate enzyme-catalysed reactions.

However, temperatures above an enzyme's optimum may destabilise its three-dimensional structure.

Disruption of non-covalent interactions, including hydrogen bonds, ionic interactions, and hydrophobic interactions, can alter the active-site structure and reduce catalytic activity.

The temperature at which an enzyme exhibits maximum activity depends on its molecular properties and biological environment.

### Evaluation of Alternative Answers

- A. Incorrect. Substrate concentration does not necessarily decrease as temperature increases.
- C. Incorrect. High temperatures do not necessarily increase enzyme-substrate affinity and may disrupt substrate recognition.
- D. Incorrect. Many enzymes remain active above 25°C, depending on their biological source and temperature stability.

## Question 8: Cofactors and Coenzymes

Which statement best distinguishes a coenzyme from a metal-ion cofactor?

A. A coenzyme is an organic non-protein molecule that assists enzyme catalysis, whereas a metal-ion cofactor is an inorganic component required by certain enzymes.  
B. A coenzyme is always a protein, whereas a metal-ion cofactor is always organic.  
C. Coenzymes permanently destroy enzymes after catalysis.  
D. Metal-ion cofactors are exclusively involved in enzyme inhibition.

**Correct Answer: A. A coenzyme is an organic non-protein molecule that assists enzyme catalysis, whereas a metal-ion cofactor is an inorganic component required by certain enzymes.**

### Scientific Explanation

Some enzymes require additional chemical components known as cofactors for catalytic activity.

Cofactors may include:

- Metal ions, such as Zn2+, Mg2+, and Fe2+.
- Organic molecules known as coenzymes, such as NAD+, FAD, and coenzyme A.

Coenzymes frequently participate in the transfer of electrons, chemical groups, or atoms during enzymatic reactions.

Some cofactors bind loosely, while others are tightly associated with the enzyme as prosthetic groups.

### Evaluation of Alternative Answers

- B. Incorrect. Coenzymes are organic non-protein molecules, not proteins.
- C. Incorrect. Coenzymes participate in catalytic processes and are not defined by enzyme destruction.
- D. Incorrect. Metal-ion cofactors commonly support catalysis rather than exclusively inhibiting enzymes.

## Question 9: Effect of pH on Enzyme Activity

How can changes in pH influence enzyme catalytic activity?

A. By changing the ionisation state of amino acid residues involved in catalysis and substrate binding  
B. By changing the genetic sequence of every enzyme molecule  
C. By permanently increasing the enzyme concentration  
D. By converting every enzyme into a non-protein molecule

**Correct Answer: A. By changing the ionisation state of amino acid residues involved in catalysis and substrate binding**

### Scientific Explanation

The catalytic activity of many enzymes depends on the ionisation state of specific amino acid residues within their active sites.

Changes in pH can alter the protonation and deprotonation states of these residues.

This may affect:

- Substrate binding.
- Catalytic acid-base reactions.
- Electrostatic interactions.
- Protein conformation and structural stability.

Each enzyme has a characteristic pH range within which it exhibits optimal activity.

Extreme pH conditions may also cause irreversible structural changes or denaturation.

### Evaluation of Alternative Answers

- B. Incorrect. Changes in pH do not ordinarily alter the genetic sequence encoding an enzyme.
- C. Incorrect. pH does not directly increase the concentration of enzyme molecules.
- D. Incorrect. Enzymes do not become non-protein molecules simply because pH changes.

## Question 10: Enzyme Kinetic Calculation

An enzyme has a Vmax of 120 µmol/min and a Km of 4 mmol/L. Assuming Michaelis-Menten kinetics, calculate the initial reaction velocity when the substrate concentration is 12 mmol/L.

A. 30 µmol/min  
B. 60 µmol/min  
C. 90 µmol/min  
D. 120 µmol/min

**Correct Answer: C. 90 µmol/min**

### Scientific Explanation

The Michaelis-Menten equation is:

v = (Vmax × [S]) / (Km + [S])

Given:

- Vmax = 120 µmol/min
- Km = 4 mmol/L
- [S] = 12 mmol/L

Substituting:

v = (120 × 12) / (4 + 12)

v = 1440 / 16

**v = 90 µmol/min**

Therefore, the initial reaction velocity is 90 µmol/min.

### Evaluation of Alternative Answers

- A. Incorrect. This value results from an incorrect application of the Michaelis-Menten equation.
- B. Incorrect. This is the velocity expected when substrate concentration equals Km.
- C. Correct. The calculated velocity is 90 µmol/min.
- D. Incorrect. Vmax is approached at very high substrate concentrations; it is not the expected velocity at 12 mmol/L.
