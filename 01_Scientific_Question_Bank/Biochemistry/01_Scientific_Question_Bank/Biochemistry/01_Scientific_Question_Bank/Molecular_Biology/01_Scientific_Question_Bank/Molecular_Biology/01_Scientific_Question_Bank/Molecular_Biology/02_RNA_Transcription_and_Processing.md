
# RNA Transcription and Processing

## Introduction

This question set assesses advanced understanding of RNA synthesis, transcriptional regulation, RNA polymerases, RNA processing, and post-transcriptional gene regulation in eukaryotic and prokaryotic cells.

Each question includes the correct answer, a detailed scientific explanation, and an evaluation of the alternative options.

## Question 1: RNA Polymerase Function

Which enzyme is primarily responsible for synthesising messenger RNA (mRNA) from a DNA template in eukaryotic cells?

A. DNA polymerase I  
B. RNA polymerase I  
C. RNA polymerase II  
D. RNA polymerase III

**Correct Answer: C. RNA polymerase II**

### Scientific Explanation

Eukaryotic cells contain three major nuclear RNA polymerases with distinct transcriptional functions.

- RNA polymerase I primarily synthesises the large precursor RNA that gives rise to 18S, 5.8S, and 28S ribosomal RNAs.
- RNA polymerase II synthesises pre-mRNA, which is processed into mature mRNA, as well as several non-coding RNAs.
- RNA polymerase III synthesises transfer RNA (tRNA), 5S ribosomal RNA, and other small RNAs.

RNA polymerase II therefore performs the major transcriptional role in protein-coding gene expression.

### Evaluation of Alternative Answers

- A. Incorrect. DNA polymerase I is a bacterial DNA-processing enzyme involved in DNA replication and repair.
- B. Incorrect. RNA polymerase I primarily synthesises large precursor rRNA.
- D. Incorrect. RNA polymerase III primarily synthesises tRNA and 5S rRNA.

## Question 2: Transcription Initiation

What is the primary function of the TATA-binding protein (TBP) during transcription initiation in many eukaryotic protein-coding genes?

A. It directly synthesises the RNA transcript.  
B. It recognises and binds the TATA box as part of the transcription initiation machinery.  
C. It removes introns from pre-mRNA.  
D. It adds the poly(A) tail to mature mRNA.

**Correct Answer: B. It recognises and binds the TATA box as part of the transcription initiation machinery.**

### Scientific Explanation

The TATA-binding protein is a component of the general transcription factor complex TFIID.

For genes containing a TATA box, TBP recognises and binds this promoter element, helping recruit and organise additional transcription factors and RNA polymerase II at the promoter.

TBP bends the DNA upon binding, contributing to assembly of the pre-initiation complex.

Not all eukaryotic protein-coding gene promoters contain a TATA box; some use other core promoter elements.

### Evaluation of Alternative Answers

- A. Incorrect. RNA polymerase II catalyses RNA synthesis.
- C. Incorrect. The spliceosome performs pre-mRNA splicing.
- D. Incorrect. The cleavage and polyadenylation machinery processes the 3′ end of pre-mRNA.

## Question 3: RNA Synthesis Direction

In which direction is a newly synthesised RNA molecule elongated during transcription?

A. 3′ to 5′  
B. 5′ to 3′  
C. Both directions simultaneously  
D. Direction varies according to the nucleotide sequence

**Correct Answer: B. 5′ to 3′**

### Scientific Explanation

RNA polymerases catalyse the addition of ribonucleoside triphosphates to the 3′-hydroxyl group of the growing RNA molecule.

Consequently, RNA synthesis proceeds in the 5′ to 3′ direction.

The DNA template strand is read in the 3′ to 5′ direction.

The resulting RNA transcript is complementary and antiparallel to the DNA template strand, with uracil replacing thymine in RNA.

### Evaluation of Alternative Answers

- A. Incorrect. This is the direction in which the DNA template is read, not the direction of RNA synthesis.
- C. Incorrect. RNA polymerases extend RNA in the 5′ to 3′ direction.
- D. Incorrect. RNA sequence does not change the fundamental directionality of RNA polymerisation.

## Question 4: RNA Splicing

Which molecular complex is primarily responsible for removing introns from most eukaryotic pre-mRNA transcripts?

A. Ribosome  
B. Spliceosome  
C. Proteasome  
D. Replisome

**Correct Answer: B. Spliceosome**

### Scientific Explanation

The spliceosome is a large ribonucleoprotein complex responsible for removing introns from most eukaryotic pre-mRNA molecules.

It consists of small nuclear ribonucleoproteins (snRNPs), including U1, U2, U4, U5, and U6, together with associated proteins.

During conventional pre-mRNA splicing:

1. The spliceosome recognises the 5′ splice site, branch point, and 3′ splice site.
2. The 2′-hydroxyl group of the branch-point adenosine participates in the first transesterification reaction.
3. A lariat-shaped intron intermediate is formed.
4. The second transesterification reaction joins the neighbouring exons.
5. The intron lariat is released and subsequently degraded.

### Evaluation of Alternative Answers

- A. Incorrect. Ribosomes translate mRNA into proteins.
- C. Incorrect. Proteasomes degrade proteins.
- D. Incorrect. The replisome is the protein machinery responsible for DNA replication.

## Question 5: 5′ Capping of Eukaryotic mRNA

What is the principal function of the 5′ cap added to many eukaryotic mRNA molecules?

A. To initiate DNA replication  
B. To protect mRNA from degradation and assist translation initiation  
C. To remove all introns from pre-mRNA  
D. To terminate transcription immediately

**Correct Answer: B. To protect mRNA from degradation and assist translation initiation**

### Scientific Explanation

The 5′ cap is a modified guanine nucleotide, usually 7-methylguanosine, attached to the first nucleotide of a eukaryotic mRNA through an unusual 5′–5′ triphosphate linkage.

Capping occurs co-transcriptionally, generally when the nascent RNA is relatively short.

Important functions include:

- Protecting the RNA from degradation by 5′ exonucleases.
- Promoting recognition of mRNA by translation initiation factors.
- Supporting nuclear processing and export.
- Contributing to efficient gene expression.

The cap-binding complex participates in nuclear RNA processing, while the eukaryotic translation initiation factor eIF4E recognises the cap during translation initiation in the cytoplasm.

### Evaluation of Alternative Answers

- A. Incorrect. The 5′ cap is involved in mRNA processing and expression, not DNA replication.
- C. Incorrect. Introns are removed primarily by the spliceosome.
- D. Incorrect. Capping does not immediately terminate transcription.

## Question 6: Polyadenylation

Which statement best describes the role of the poly(A) tail in eukaryotic mRNA?

A. It serves as the template for DNA synthesis.  
B. It contributes to mRNA stability, nuclear export, and translation efficiency.  
C. It converts mRNA into rRNA.  
D. It directly removes introns from pre-mRNA.

**Correct Answer: B. It contributes to mRNA stability, nuclear export, and translation efficiency.**

### Scientific Explanation

Most eukaryotic mRNAs undergo cleavage at their 3′ ends followed by the addition of a poly(A) tail by poly(A) polymerase.

The poly(A) tail is bound by poly(A)-binding proteins, which contribute to several aspects of mRNA metabolism.

Its functions include:

- Increasing mRNA stability.
- Supporting nuclear export.
- Enhancing translation efficiency.
- Participating in regulation of mRNA degradation.

The poly(A) tail is not generally encoded directly as a continuous sequence in the DNA template; it is added enzymatically during RNA processing.

### Evaluation of Alternative Answers

- A. Incorrect. The poly(A) tail is not a DNA replication template.
- C. Incorrect. mRNA does not become rRNA through polyadenylation.
- D. Incorrect. Intron removal is performed by the spliceosome.

## Question 7: Alternative Splicing

What is the biological significance of alternative splicing in eukaryotic gene expression?

A. It allows one pre-mRNA transcript to produce different mature mRNA isoforms.  
B. It permanently converts RNA into DNA.  
C. It prevents all protein synthesis.  
D. It removes every exon from a pre-mRNA molecule.

**Correct Answer: A. It allows one pre-mRNA transcript to produce different mature mRNA isoforms.**

### Scientific Explanation

Alternative splicing is a post-transcriptional process in which different combinations of exons are retained in mature RNA transcripts derived from the same pre-mRNA.

Common patterns include:

- Exon skipping.
- Alternative 5′ splice-site selection.
- Alternative 3′ splice-site selection.
- Mutually exclusive exons.
- Intron retention.

Alternative splicing expands transcript and protein diversity without requiring a corresponding increase in the number of genes.

Its regulation depends on splice-site sequences and RNA-binding regulatory proteins, including serine/arginine-rich proteins and heterogeneous nuclear ribonucleoproteins.

### Evaluation of Alternative Answers

- B. Incorrect. RNA-to-DNA synthesis is reverse transcription, not alternative splicing.
- C. Incorrect. Alternative splicing can generate transcripts that are translated into different protein isoforms.
- D. Incorrect. Exons are generally retained in mature mRNA, although their combinations can vary.

## Question 8: Transcription Termination in Eukaryotes

Which event is closely associated with the termination and 3′-end processing of many RNA polymerase II transcripts?

A. Recognition of a polyadenylation signal followed by RNA cleavage and polyadenylation  
B. Removal of DNA primers by DNA polymerase  
C. Formation of Okazaki fragments  
D. Direct degradation of the entire chromosome

**Correct Answer: A. Recognition of a polyadenylation signal followed by RNA cleavage and polyadenylation**

### Scientific Explanation

Many RNA polymerase II transcripts contain a polyadenylation signal, commonly represented by the sequence AAUAAA or a close variant.

This signal is recognised by the cleavage and polyadenylation machinery.

The pre-mRNA is cleaved downstream of the signal, and poly(A) polymerase adds a poly(A) tail to the newly generated 3′ end.

RNA polymerase II subsequently terminates transcription through mechanisms involving RNA processing and associated termination factors.

A key mechanism is the torpedo model, in which an exonuclease degrades the downstream RNA remaining associated with the polymerase and contributes to transcription termination.

### Evaluation of Alternative Answers

- B. Incorrect. DNA primer removal is part of DNA replication.
- C. Incorrect. Okazaki fragments are produced during discontinuous DNA replication.
- D. Incorrect. Transcription termination does not involve chromosome degradation.

## Question 9: MicroRNA and Gene Regulation

How do microRNAs (miRNAs) commonly regulate gene expression in animal cells?

A. By binding target RNA molecules through complementary base pairing and promoting translational repression or mRNA destabilisation  
B. By directly synthesising proteins  
C. By replacing DNA polymerase during replication  
D. By permanently converting mRNA into genomic DNA

**Correct Answer: A. By binding target RNA molecules through complementary base pairing and promoting translational repression or mRNA destabilisation**

### Scientific Explanation

MicroRNAs are short, non-coding RNA molecules that regulate gene expression after transcription.

Mature miRNAs are incorporated into the RNA-induced silencing complex (RISC), which contains Argonaute proteins.

The miRNA guides this complex to target mRNA molecules through sequence complementarity, often involving the miRNA seed region.

In animals, imperfect base pairing commonly results in:

- Translational repression.
- Deadenylation.
- mRNA destabilisation and degradation.

Some miRNAs can also direct mRNA cleavage when extensive complementarity is present.

### Evaluation of Alternative Answers

- B. Incorrect. Ribosomes synthesise proteins; miRNAs regulate gene expression.
- C. Incorrect. DNA polymerases perform DNA replication.
- D. Incorrect. Conversion of RNA into DNA is carried out by reverse transcriptase in specific biological systems.

## Question 10: Prokaryotic Transcription and Translation

Why can transcription and translation occur simultaneously in many bacterial cells?

A. Bacteria have a nuclear envelope that separates the processes.  
B. Bacteria generally lack a membrane-bound nucleus, allowing ribosomes to access nascent mRNA while it is being transcribed.  
C. Bacterial ribosomes are located inside mitochondria.  
D. Bacterial RNA polymerase synthesises proteins directly.

**Correct Answer: B. Bacteria generally lack a membrane-bound nucleus, allowing ribosomes to access nascent mRNA while it is being transcribed.**

### Scientific Explanation

In bacteria, transcription occurs in the cytoplasmic region of the cell because they lack a membrane-bound nucleus.

Ribosomes can therefore associate with a nascent mRNA molecule while RNA polymerase is still synthesising it.

This coupling enables translation to begin before transcription has been completed.

In eukaryotic cells, nuclear transcription and cytoplasmic translation are spatially separated. Eukaryotic pre-mRNA must undergo processing and export before conventional cytoplasmic translation.

### Evaluation of Alternative Answers

- A. Incorrect. Bacteria generally lack a membrane-bound nucleus.
- C. Incorrect. Bacteria do not possess mitochondria.
- D. Incorrect. RNA polymerase synthesises RNA, whereas ribosomes synthesise proteins.
