
# Protein Synthesis and Translation

## Overview

This question set assesses intermediate-to-advanced understanding of protein synthesis, with emphasis on the molecular mechanisms of translation, ribosomal function, transfer RNA, genetic code interpretation, and regulation of protein synthesis.

---

## Question 1: Role of Ribosomes in Translation

**Question:** Which of the following best describes the primary role of ribosomes during protein synthesis?

A. Replication of DNA  
B. Synthesis of messenger RNA  
C. Translation of mRNA into a polypeptide  
D. Modification of DNA bases  

**Correct Answer:** C. Translation of mRNA into a polypeptide.

**Explanation:** Ribosomes are the molecular machinery responsible for translating the nucleotide sequence of mRNA into the amino acid sequence of a protein. They coordinate interactions among mRNA, transfer RNA (tRNA), and amino acids. The small ribosomal subunit participates in mRNA binding and decoding, while the large subunit catalyses peptide bond formation.

**Distractor Evaluation:**
- A is incorrect because DNA replication is carried out by DNA polymerases and associated replication proteins.
- B is incorrect because RNA polymerases synthesise RNA from DNA templates.
- D is incorrect because DNA base modification is carried out by specialised enzymes.

## Question 2: Aminoacyl-tRNA Synthetases

**Question:** What is the primary function of aminoacyl-tRNA synthetases?

A. Catalyse peptide bond formation  
B. Attach the correct amino acid to its corresponding tRNA  
C. Remove introns from pre-mRNA  
D. Facilitate ribosomal translocation  

**Correct Answer:** B. Attach the correct amino acid to its corresponding tRNA.

**Explanation:** Aminoacyl-tRNA synthetases catalyse the attachment of specific amino acids to their corresponding tRNAs. This process, known as tRNA charging, uses ATP. Accurate aminoacylation is essential for translation fidelity because the ribosome generally checks codon–anticodon pairing rather than independently verifying the amino acid attached to each tRNA.

**Distractor Evaluation:**
- A is incorrect because peptide bond formation is catalysed by the peptidyl transferase centre of the large ribosomal subunit.
- C is incorrect because pre-mRNA splicing is performed by the spliceosome.
- D is incorrect because translocation involves ribosomal factors such as elongation factor G in bacteria and eEF2 in eukaryotes.

## Question 3: Direction of Translation

**Question:** In which direction does a ribosome move along an mRNA molecule during translation?

A. 3′ to 5′  
B. 5′ to 3′  
C. N-terminus to C-terminus  
D. C-terminus to N-terminus  

**Correct Answer:** B. 5′ to 3′.

**Explanation:** Ribosomes read mRNA in the 5′ to 3′ direction. As translation proceeds, amino acids are added to the growing polypeptide at its carboxyl (C-) terminus. Consequently, the polypeptide is synthesised from the N-terminus towards the C-terminus.

**Distractor Evaluation:**
- A is incorrect because ribosomes do not read mRNA in the 3′ to 5′ direction.
- C describes the direction of polypeptide elongation rather than ribosomal movement along mRNA.
- D is incorrect because polypeptide synthesis proceeds from the N-terminus towards the C-terminus.

## Question 4: Initiation of Translation in Eukaryotes

**Question:** Which sequence best describes the initial stages of cap-dependent translation initiation in eukaryotic cells?

A. Large ribosomal subunit binds first, followed by DNA attachment.  
B. The small ribosomal subunit, initiation factors and initiator tRNA assemble and are recruited to the mRNA.  
C. RNA polymerase binds directly to the ribosome.  
D. The ribosome begins translation at the first nucleotide of the mRNA.

**Correct Answer:** B. The small ribosomal subunit, initiation factors and initiator tRNA assemble and are recruited to the mRNA.

**Explanation:** During cap-dependent initiation, initiation factors help recruit the small ribosomal subunit and initiator methionyl-tRNA to the 5′ end of the mRNA. The initiation complex scans the mRNA to identify an appropriate start codon, commonly AUG in a suitable Kozak sequence context. The large ribosomal subunit subsequently joins to form the functional ribosome.

**Distractor Evaluation:**
- A is incorrect because the small subunit participates in the initial initiation complex.
- C is incorrect because RNA polymerase functions in transcription, not direct ribosome recruitment.
- D is incorrect because translation generally begins at an appropriate start codon rather than the first nucleotide.

## Question 5: Peptide Bond Formation

**Question:** Which component of the ribosome is primarily responsible for catalysing peptide bond formation?

A. Small ribosomal subunit  
B. Large ribosomal subunit  
C. mRNA cap-binding complex  
D. Aminoacyl-tRNA synthetase  

**Correct Answer:** B. Large ribosomal subunit.

**Explanation:** The peptidyl transferase centre, located in the large ribosomal subunit, catalyses peptide bond formation. This catalytic activity is primarily mediated by ribosomal RNA, demonstrating the ribozyme nature of the ribosome.

**Distractor Evaluation:**
- A is incorrect because the small subunit primarily participates in mRNA binding and codon decoding.
- C is incorrect because the cap-binding complex participates in eukaryotic translation initiation.
- D is incorrect because aminoacyl-tRNA synthetases attach amino acids to tRNAs.

## Question 6: Wobble Hypothesis

**Question:** What is the biological significance of the wobble hypothesis in translation?

A. It explains why all amino acids have only one codon.  
B. It allows certain tRNAs to recognise more than one synonymous codon.  
C. It prevents mutations in DNA.  
D. It explains the formation of peptide bonds.

**Correct Answer:** B. It allows certain tRNAs to recognise more than one synonymous codon.

**Explanation:** The wobble hypothesis describes flexible base pairing between the third nucleotide of an mRNA codon and the first nucleotide of its corresponding tRNA anticodon. This flexibility allows a single tRNA species to recognise multiple synonymous codons, contributing to the degeneracy of the genetic code.

**Distractor Evaluation:**
- A is incorrect because most amino acids are encoded by multiple synonymous codons.
- C is incorrect because the wobble hypothesis does not prevent DNA mutations.
- D is incorrect because peptide bond formation is catalysed by the ribosomal peptidyl transferase centre.

## Question 7: Translation Termination

**Question:** What happens when a ribosome encounters a stop codon during translation?

A. A special tRNA carrying a stop amino acid enters the A site.  
B. A release factor recognises the stop codon and promotes polypeptide release.  
C. DNA polymerase binds to the ribosome.  
D. The ribosome continues translating until the end of the mRNA.

**Correct Answer:** B. A release factor recognises the stop codon and promotes polypeptide release.

**Explanation:** Stop codons (UAA, UAG and UGA) do not normally encode amino acids. Instead, they are recognised by protein release factors. These factors promote hydrolysis of the bond linking the completed polypeptide to the tRNA in the ribosomal P site, leading to polypeptide release and subsequent ribosome recycling.

**Distractor Evaluation:**
- A is incorrect because stop codons are not recognised by conventional aminoacyl-tRNAs.
- C is incorrect because DNA polymerase functions in DNA replication.
- D is incorrect because translation terminates when a stop codon is recognised.

## Question 8: Polysomes

**Question:** What is the major advantage of polysome formation during protein synthesis?

A. It prevents mRNA degradation completely.  
B. It allows multiple ribosomes to translate a single mRNA molecule simultaneously.  
C. It increases the rate of DNA replication.  
D. It prevents amino acid misincorporation.

**Correct Answer:** B. It allows multiple ribosomes to translate a single mRNA molecule simultaneously.

**Explanation:** A polysome, or polyribosome, consists of multiple ribosomes translating the same mRNA molecule at the same time. This arrangement increases the efficiency and output of protein synthesis from an individual mRNA transcript.

**Distractor Evaluation:**
- A is incorrect because polysomes do not completely prevent mRNA degradation.
- C is incorrect because polysomes are involved in translation, not DNA replication.
- D is incorrect because translation fidelity depends on several mechanisms, including accurate tRNA charging and codon–anticodon recognition.

## Question 9: Regulation of Translation by microRNAs

**Question:** How do microRNAs (miRNAs) commonly regulate protein expression in eukaryotic cells?

A. By directly increasing DNA replication  
B. By binding to complementary sequences in target mRNAs and promoting translational repression or mRNA destabilisation  
C. By converting mRNA into DNA  
D. By catalysing peptide bond formation

**Correct Answer:** B. By binding to complementary sequences in target mRNAs and promoting translational repression or mRNA destabilisation.

**Explanation:** MicroRNAs are small non-coding RNAs that associate with Argonaute-containing RNA-induced silencing complexes (RISC). Through sequence complementarity, they guide these complexes to target mRNAs, commonly resulting in translational repression, deadenylation, decapping and mRNA degradation. In animals, miRNA-target interactions frequently involve imperfect base pairing.

**Distractor Evaluation:**
- A is incorrect because miRNAs regulate gene expression rather than directly promoting DNA replication.
- C is incorrect because conversion of RNA into DNA is performed by reverse transcriptases.
- D is incorrect because peptide bond formation is catalysed by the ribosome.

## Question 10: Differences Between Prokaryotic and Eukaryotic Translation

**Question:** Which statement correctly describes a major difference between prokaryotic and eukaryotic protein synthesis?

A. Prokaryotes use 80S ribosomes, whereas eukaryotes use 70S ribosomes.  
B. Translation and transcription can be coupled in prokaryotes, whereas they are spatially separated in typical eukaryotic cells.  
C. Eukaryotes do not require tRNA during translation.  
D. Prokaryotes synthesise proteins without ribosomes.

**Correct Answer:** B. Translation and transcription can be coupled in prokaryotes, whereas they are spatially separated in typical eukaryotic cells.

**Explanation:** Prokaryotes lack a membrane-bound nucleus, allowing ribosomes to begin translating an mRNA while it is still being transcribed. In typical eukaryotic cells, transcription occurs in the nucleus, while translation occurs mainly in the cytoplasm or on the rough endoplasmic reticulum. Prokaryotic cytoplasmic ribosomes are generally 70S, whereas eukaryotic cytoplasmic ribosomes are generally 80S.

**Distractor Evaluation:**
- A is incorrect because the ribosomal sizes are reversed.
- C is incorrect because tRNA is essential for translation in both prokaryotes and eukaryotes.
- D is incorrect because prokaryotes require ribosomes for protein synthesis.
