
# Enzymology and Enzyme Kinetics

## Section Overview

This question set assesses understanding of enzyme structure, catalytic mechanisms, enzyme kinetics, inhibition, regulation, and factors affecting enzyme activity.

The questions are designed at intermediate-to-advanced undergraduate and postgraduate levels.

## Question 1: Michaelis–Menten Kinetics

An enzyme-catalysed reaction follows Michaelis–Menten kinetics. Which statement best describes the Michaelis constant (Km)?

A. The maximum velocity achieved when the enzyme is saturated with substrate.

B. The substrate concentration at which the reaction velocity is half of Vmax.

C. The rate constant for enzyme denaturation.

D. The concentration of enzyme required to initiate a reaction.

**Correct Answer: B**

**Explanation:**

Km is the substrate concentration at which the initial reaction velocity is half of the maximum velocity (Vmax), under Michaelis–Menten conditions.

A lower Km often indicates a higher apparent affinity of an enzyme for its substrate, although Km is not universally equivalent to the substrate dissociation constant.

**Evaluation of Options:**

- A: Incorrect. This describes Vmax.
- B: Correct. It is the defining relationship for Km.
- C: Incorrect. Km does not measure enzyme denaturation.
- D: Incorrect. Km refers to substrate concentration, not enzyme concentration.

## Question 2: Competitive Inhibition

What is the principal effect of a competitive inhibitor on the kinetic parameters of an enzyme?

A. Decreases Vmax without changing Km.

B. Increases apparent Km without changing Vmax.

C. Decreases both Km and Vmax.

D. Increases Vmax and decreases Km.

**Correct Answer: B**

**Explanation:**

A competitive inhibitor competes with the substrate for binding to the enzyme's active site. Increasing substrate concentration can overcome this inhibition.

Consequently, the apparent Km increases, while Vmax remains unchanged.

**Evaluation of Options:**

- A: Incorrect. This is not the characteristic effect of competitive inhibition.
- B: Correct. Apparent Km increases while Vmax remains unchanged.
- C: Incorrect. Both parameters do not decrease in classical competitive inhibition.
- D: Incorrect. Competitive inhibition does not increase Vmax.

## Question 3: Non-Competitive Inhibition

In classical pure non-competitive inhibition, what happens to Vmax and Km?

A. Vmax decreases while Km remains unchanged.

B. Vmax increases while Km decreases.

C. Both Vmax and Km increase.

D. Vmax remains unchanged while Km increases.

**Correct Answer: A**

**Explanation:**

A pure non-competitive inhibitor binds equally well to the free enzyme and the enzyme–substrate complex at a site distinct from the active site.

This reduces the concentration of functional enzyme available for catalysis, thereby decreasing Vmax without changing Km.

**Evaluation of Options:**

- A: Correct. This describes classical pure non-competitive inhibition.
- B: Incorrect. Neither parameter changes in this direction.
- C: Incorrect. Pure non-competitive inhibition decreases Vmax.
- D: Incorrect. This is not the characteristic pattern of pure non-competitive inhibition.

## Question 4: Uncompetitive Inhibition

Which kinetic pattern is associated with uncompetitive inhibition?

A. Increased Km and unchanged Vmax.

B. Decreased Km and decreased Vmax.

C. Unchanged Km and decreased Vmax.

D. Increased Km and increased Vmax.

**Correct Answer: B**

**Explanation:**

An uncompetitive inhibitor binds exclusively to the enzyme–substrate complex.

This stabilises the enzyme–substrate–inhibitor complex and reduces both the apparent Km and Vmax by the same proportional factor.

**Evaluation of Options:**

- A: Incorrect. This is characteristic of competitive inhibition.
- B: Correct. Both apparent Km and Vmax decrease.
- C: Incorrect. This describes pure non-competitive inhibition.
- D: Incorrect. This is not associated with uncompetitive inhibition.

## Question 5: Enzyme Specificity

Which property enables an enzyme to selectively recognise and catalyse the reaction of a particular substrate?

A. Random molecular collision.

B. Specific structural and chemical complementarity between the substrate and active site.

C. Complete destruction of the substrate before binding.

D. Permanent alteration of the enzyme's primary structure.

**Correct Answer: B**

**Explanation:**

Enzyme specificity is largely determined by the structural and chemical characteristics of the active site.

The active site contains amino acid residues that interact with the substrate through forces such as hydrogen bonding, ionic interactions, hydrophobic interactions and van der Waals forces.

The induced-fit model also explains how substrate binding can promote conformational changes that facilitate catalysis.

**Evaluation of Options:**

- A: Incorrect. Molecular collisions contribute to enzyme–substrate encounters but do not explain specificity.
- B: Correct. Active-site complementarity is central to substrate recognition.
- C: Incorrect. Substrate destruction is not required for binding.
- D: Incorrect. Permanent alteration of primary structure is not the basis of normal enzyme specificity.

## Question 6: Allosteric Regulation

What distinguishes an allosteric enzyme from a typical Michaelis–Menten enzyme?

A. It cannot be regulated by metabolites.

B. It commonly exhibits cooperative substrate binding and a sigmoidal velocity–substrate curve.

C. It always follows first-order kinetics.

D. It has no regulatory binding sites.

**Correct Answer: B**

**Explanation:**

Allosteric enzymes possess regulatory sites that are distinct from their catalytic sites. Binding of an effector molecule can alter enzyme conformation and activity.

Many allosteric enzymes exhibit cooperative substrate binding, producing a sigmoidal rather than a hyperbolic velocity–substrate relationship.

**Evaluation of Options:**

- A: Incorrect. Allosteric enzymes are commonly regulated by metabolites.
- B: Correct. Cooperativity and sigmoidal kinetics are characteristic features of many allosteric enzymes.
- C: Incorrect. Their kinetic behaviour is more complex than a universal first-order model.
- D: Incorrect. Regulatory binding sites are central to allosteric regulation.

## Question 7: Enzyme Cofactors

Which of the following best describes a coenzyme?

A. An inorganic ion that permanently destroys enzyme activity.

B. An organic, non-protein molecule that assists enzyme-catalysed reactions.

C. A protein that always forms the active site of an enzyme.

D. A substrate that cannot undergo chemical transformation.

**Correct Answer: B**

**Explanation:**

A coenzyme is an organic non-protein molecule that assists enzyme catalysis. Many coenzymes are derived from vitamins and participate in transferring electrons or chemical groups.

Examples include NAD+, FAD and coenzyme A.

**Evaluation of Options:**

- A: Incorrect. Inorganic ions may act as cofactors but do not necessarily destroy enzyme activity.
- B: Correct. This is the definition of a coenzyme.
- C: Incorrect. A coenzyme is not a protein.
- D: Incorrect. Coenzymes participate in biochemical transformations.

## Question 8: Temperature and Enzyme Activity

Why does enzyme activity generally decrease at temperatures above an enzyme's optimum temperature?

A. Substrate concentration always becomes zero.

B. Increased thermal energy can disrupt interactions that maintain the enzyme's functional structure.

C. Enzymes are converted into carbohydrates.

D. The enzyme's molecular mass permanently increases.

**Correct Answer: B**

**Explanation:**

Enzymes are proteins whose catalytic activity depends on their three-dimensional structures.

Excessive temperature can disrupt non-covalent interactions that stabilise protein conformation, resulting in partial or extensive denaturation and loss of catalytic activity.

**Evaluation of Options:**

- A: Incorrect. Substrate concentration does not necessarily become zero.
- B: Correct. Heat-induced structural changes can reduce enzyme activity.
- C: Incorrect. Enzymes are not converted into carbohydrates.
- D: Incorrect. Increased molecular mass is not the explanation.

## Question 9: Lineweaver–Burk Plot

Which statement correctly describes a Lineweaver–Burk plot?

A. It plots reaction velocity against enzyme concentration.

B. It plots the reciprocal of initial velocity against the reciprocal of substrate concentration.

C. It plots substrate concentration against temperature.

D. It measures protein concentration directly.

**Correct Answer: B**

**Explanation:**

The Lineweaver–Burk plot is a double-reciprocal representation of the Michaelis–Menten equation.

It plots 1/V against 1/[S], where V is the initial reaction velocity and [S] is the substrate concentration.

The y-intercept represents 1/Vmax, while the x-intercept represents -1/Km.

Although historically important, reciprocal transformations can magnify experimental errors at low substrate concentrations. Nonlinear regression is generally preferred for estimating kinetic parameters from experimental data.

**Evaluation of Options:**

- A: Incorrect. This is not the definition of a Lineweaver–Burk plot.
- B: Correct. It represents reciprocal velocity against reciprocal substrate concentration.
- C: Incorrect. Temperature is not the defining variable.
- D: Incorrect. It is not a direct protein concentration assay.

## Question 10: Enzyme Turnover Number

What does the turnover number (kcat) represent?

A. The number of enzyme molecules in a reaction mixture.

B. The number of substrate molecules converted into product per enzyme active site per unit time under saturating substrate conditions.

C. The concentration of inhibitor required to denature an enzyme.

D. The substrate concentration at which an enzyme becomes inactive.

**Correct Answer: B**

**Explanation:**

The turnover number, kcat, represents the maximum number of catalytic cycles completed by an enzyme active site per unit time when the enzyme is saturated with substrate.

It is calculated as:

kcat = Vmax / [E]total

where:

- Vmax is the maximum reaction velocity.
- [E]total is the total concentration of enzyme active sites.

The catalytic efficiency of an enzyme can be evaluated using the ratio kcat/Km.

**Evaluation of Options:**

- A: Incorrect. This describes neither turnover number nor catalytic activity.
- B: Correct. It defines kcat.
- C: Incorrect. This is unrelated to turnover number.
- D: Incorrect. This does not describe kcat.
