
# Evaluation 02: Glycolysis and Gluconeogenesis

## 1. Scientific Question

Explain the major stages of glycolysis and gluconeogenesis, identify their key regulatory enzymes, and describe how these two metabolic pathways are reciprocally regulated.

## 2. Illustrative AI-Generated Answer

Glycolysis is a metabolic pathway in which glucose is broken down into two molecules of pyruvate through a series of ten enzyme-catalysed reactions. It occurs in the cytoplasm and produces a net gain of two ATP molecules and two NADH molecules per glucose molecule.

The three major irreversible regulatory steps of glycolysis are catalysed by:

1. Hexokinase or glucokinase.
2. Phosphofructokinase-1 (PFK-1).
3. Pyruvate kinase.

Gluconeogenesis is the synthesis of glucose from non-carbohydrate precursors, including lactate, glycerol, and glucogenic amino acids. It occurs mainly in the liver and, during prolonged fasting, also in the kidneys.

Gluconeogenesis bypasses the three irreversible steps of glycolysis through four enzymes:

1. Pyruvate carboxylase.
2. Phosphoenolpyruvate carboxykinase (PEPCK).
3. Fructose-1,6-bisphosphatase.
4. Glucose-6-phosphatase.

The two pathways are reciprocally regulated to prevent excessive simultaneous activity and unnecessary energy consumption.

## 4. Scientific Verification

### Glycolysis

Glycolysis consists of ten reactions divided into two broad phases:

- Energy investment phase: Two ATP molecules are consumed.
- Energy payoff phase: Four ATP molecules and two NADH molecules are produced.

The overall net yield per glucose molecule is:

- 2 pyruvate molecules.
- 2 ATP molecules.
- 2 NADH molecules.

The principal irreversible regulatory reactions are:

- Glucose to glucose-6-phosphate.
- Fructose-6-phosphate to fructose-1,6-bisphosphate.
- Phosphoenolpyruvate to pyruvate.

### Gluconeogenesis

Gluconeogenesis maintains blood glucose during fasting by synthesising glucose from suitable non-carbohydrate precursors.

The pathway bypasses the irreversible glycolytic reactions using:

- Pyruvate carboxylase and PEPCK.
- Fructose-1,6-bisphosphatase.
- Glucose-6-phosphatase.

The process requires energy. Conversion of two pyruvate molecules into one glucose molecule consumes four ATP, two GTP, and two NADH.

### Reciprocal Regulation

The pathways are regulated in opposite directions to coordinate glucose utilisation and glucose production.

Important regulatory factors include:

- **Fructose-2,6-bisphosphate:** Activates PFK-1 and inhibits fructose-1,6-bisphosphatase.
- **ATP:** High concentrations generally inhibit PFK-1.
- **AMP:** Activates PFK-1 and signals a low-energy state.
- **Glucagon:** In the liver, promotes conditions that favour gluconeogenesis.
- **Insulin:** Generally promotes glycolysis and suppresses hepatic gluconeogenesis.

## 5. Corrected Scientific Answer

Glycolysis is a ten-step cytosolic pathway that converts one glucose molecule into two pyruvate molecules. It produces four ATP molecules but consumes two ATP molecules, resulting in a net gain of two ATP and two NADH molecules per glucose molecule.

Its major irreversible regulatory steps are catalysed by hexokinase or glucokinase, PFK-1, and pyruvate kinase.

Gluconeogenesis synthesises glucose from non-carbohydrate precursors such as lactate, glycerol, and glucogenic amino acids. It occurs predominantly in the liver and also in the kidneys, particularly during prolonged fasting.

The pathway bypasses the three irreversible glycolytic reactions through the activities of pyruvate carboxylase, PEPCK, fructose-1,6-bisphosphatase, and glucose-6-phosphatase.

Glycolysis and gluconeogenesis are reciprocally regulated. Fructose-2,6-bisphosphate stimulates PFK-1 while inhibiting fructose-1,6-bisphosphatase. Hormonal regulation also coordinates these pathways: insulin generally favours glycolysis, whereas glucagon promotes hepatic glucose production.

The synthesis of one glucose molecule from two pyruvate molecules requires four ATP, two GTP, and two NADH molecules.
