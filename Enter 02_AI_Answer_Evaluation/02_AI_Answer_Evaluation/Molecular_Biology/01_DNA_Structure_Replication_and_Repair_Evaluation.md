## 2. Scientific Question

Explain the structural features of DNA, describe the major stages of semiconservative DNA replication, and discuss how cells maintain genomic integrity through DNA repair mechanisms.

## 3. Illustrative AI-Generated Answer

DNA is a double-stranded molecule that stores hereditary information in most living organisms. Its two strands form a double helix and run in opposite directions, with one strand oriented 5′ to 3′ and the other 3′ to 5′.

Each DNA nucleotide contains a deoxyribose sugar, a phosphate group and a nitrogenous base. Adenine pairs with thymine through two hydrogen bonds, while guanine pairs with cytosine through three hydrogen bonds. The sugar-phosphate backbone forms the external structure of the DNA molecule.

DNA replication is semiconservative because each newly formed DNA molecule contains one parental strand and one newly synthesised strand. Replication begins at specific origins where the DNA double helix is opened. Helicase separates the strands, while single-strand binding proteins help stabilise the exposed DNA.

Primase produces short RNA primers, providing free 3′-OH groups for DNA polymerases. DNA polymerases synthesise new DNA in the 5′ to 3′ direction. The leading strand is synthesised continuously, whereas the lagging strand is synthesised discontinuously as Okazaki fragments. DNA ligase joins the fragments to form a continuous strand.

DNA replication is completed when the replication machinery reaches termination regions or replication forks converge, depending on the organism and replication system.

Cells also possess DNA repair systems that correct damage and replication errors. Important mechanisms include mismatch repair, base excision repair, nucleotide excision repair and double-strand break repair. These mechanisms help preserve genetic information and reduce the accumulation of mutations.

## 6. Corrected and Improved Scientific Answer

DNA is a nucleic acid responsible for storing and transmitting hereditary information in cellular organisms. It consists of nucleotide units, each containing a deoxyribose sugar, a phosphate group and one of four nitrogenous bases: adenine, thymine, guanine or cytosine.

The two DNA strands form a double helix and are arranged antiparallel to one another. Complementary base pairing occurs through hydrogen bonds: adenine pairs with thymine through two hydrogen bonds, while guanine pairs with cytosine through three. Base-stacking interactions also contribute substantially to the stability of the double helix.

DNA replication is semiconservative, meaning that each daughter DNA molecule contains one parental strand and one newly synthesised strand. Replication begins at specific origins and involves the coordinated action of several proteins and enzymes.

Helicase unwinds the double helix, while single-strand binding proteins stabilise separated DNA strands. Topoisomerases help manage torsional stress generated during unwinding. Primase synthesises short RNA primers that provide free 3′-OH groups for DNA synthesis.

DNA polymerases extend the primers by adding nucleotides to the growing strand in the 5′ to 3′ direction. The leading strand is generally synthesised continuously, whereas the lagging strand is synthesised discontinuously as Okazaki fragments. RNA primers are removed and replaced with DNA, and DNA ligase seals the remaining nicks.

DNA replication termination differs among organisms. In eukaryotes, neighbouring replication forks commonly converge to complete replication of chromosome regions.

Genomic integrity is maintained through DNA repair pathways. Mismatch repair corrects errors that escape proofreading during replication. Base excision repair removes damaged bases, while nucleotide excision repair removes DNA segments containing certain bulky lesions. Double-strand breaks can be repaired through homologous recombination or non-homologous end joining.

Together, DNA replication fidelity, proofreading and DNA repair systems help maintain genome stability and reduce the accumulation of mutations.
