## 2. Scientific Question

Explain the process of RNA transcription, highlighting the roles of RNA polymerase, promoters and transcription factors. Discuss the major stages of eukaryotic pre-mRNA processing and explain how these processes contribute to gene expression.

## 3. Illustrative AI-Generated Answer

Transcription is the process by which genetic information stored in DNA is copied into RNA. It is an essential stage of gene expression and is catalysed by RNA polymerase.

During transcription, RNA polymerase binds to a specific DNA region called the promoter. The DNA strands separate locally, allowing one strand to serve as the template for RNA synthesis. RNA polymerase reads the DNA template in the 3′ to 5′ direction and synthesises RNA in the 5′ to 3′ direction.

In prokaryotes, a single major RNA polymerase synthesises the different classes of RNA. In eukaryotes, RNA polymerases I, II and III are responsible for synthesising different RNA types. RNA polymerase II produces most protein-coding pre-mRNA.

Eukaryotic transcription involves initiation, elongation and termination. During initiation, transcription factors and RNA polymerase assemble at the promoter. During elongation, RNA polymerase moves along the DNA template and adds complementary ribonucleotides to the growing RNA molecule. During termination, transcription ends and the RNA transcript is released.

Newly synthesised eukaryotic pre-mRNA undergoes three major processing events:

1. Addition of a 5′ cap.
2. Removal of introns and joining of exons through RNA splicing.
3. Addition of a 3′ poly(A) tail.

The 5′ cap protects the RNA and assists in translation initiation. Splicing removes non-coding introns, while the poly(A) tail contributes to RNA stability, export and translation.

These processes produce mature mRNA, which can leave the nucleus and be translated by ribosomes in the cytoplasm.

## 4. Identification of Scientific Errors and Omissions

The illustrative answer is scientifically sound overall. However, several aspects require greater precision and additional explanation.

### 4.1 Definition and Directionality of Transcription

**Assessment:** Correct.

The answer correctly defines transcription as the synthesis of RNA using DNA as a template.

It also correctly identifies the directionality of the process: RNA polymerase reads the template strand in the 3′ to 5′ direction and synthesises RNA in the 5′ to 3′ direction.

**Additional detail:** RNA polymerase does not require a pre-existing primer to initiate RNA synthesis, unlike the DNA polymerases involved in conventional DNA replication.

### 4.2 Promoters and Transcription Factors

**Assessment:** Accurate but incomplete.

The answer correctly identifies promoters as DNA regions involved in transcription initiation.

**Additional detail:** Promoter recognition differs between prokaryotes and eukaryotes.

In many bacteria, sigma factors help RNA polymerase recognise promoter sequences. In eukaryotes, general transcription factors assist in recruiting and positioning RNA polymerase II at protein-coding gene promoters.

Regulatory proteins, including activators and repressors, can also influence transcription through interactions with regulatory DNA elements.

### 4.3 RNA Polymerases

**Assessment:** Correct.

The answer correctly distinguishes the major eukaryotic RNA polymerases:

- RNA polymerase I mainly synthesises precursor ribosomal RNA.
- RNA polymerase II synthesises pre-mRNA and several non-coding RNAs.
- RNA polymerase III synthesises transfer RNA, 5S ribosomal RNA and other small RNAs.

**Additional detail:** The statement that prokaryotes possess a single major RNA polymerase is a useful generalisation for bacteria. Archaeal transcription machinery has distinct features and is more closely related in several respects to the eukaryotic system.

### 4.4 Stages of Transcription

**Assessment:** Correct but requires further explanation.

The answer identifies initiation, elongation and termination.

**Additional detail:** Transcription termination mechanisms differ between organisms. Bacterial termination may occur through intrinsic termination or factor-dependent mechanisms, while eukaryotic RNA polymerase II termination is closely associated with transcript cleavage and downstream processing events.

### 4.5 Eukaryotic Pre-mRNA Processing

**Assessment:** Scientifically accurate.

The answer correctly identifies the 5′ cap, RNA splicing and polyadenylation as major pre-mRNA processing events.

**Additional detail:**

- The 5′ cap contains a modified guanine nucleotide attached through an unusual 5′–5′ triphosphate linkage.
- Splicing is generally carried out by the spliceosome, which removes introns and joins exons.
- Alternative splicing allows different mature RNA products to be generated from the same primary transcript.
- The poly(A) tail is added after cleavage of the pre-mRNA at its 3′ end.

These processing events are coordinated with transcription and contribute to RNA stability, nuclear export and translational regulation.

### 4.6 Relationship Between RNA Processing and Gene Expression

**Assessment:** Correct but incomplete.

The answer correctly links RNA processing with mature mRNA formation and translation.

**Additional detail:** RNA processing also contributes to gene-expression regulation. Alternative splicing, RNA stability, nuclear export and RNA surveillance can influence the amount and type of protein produced from a gene.

## 6. Corrected and Improved Scientific Answer

Transcription is the process through which genetic information encoded in DNA is used to synthesise RNA. It is an essential component of gene expression and is catalysed by DNA-dependent RNA polymerases.

During transcription, RNA polymerase recognises and associates with promoter regions of DNA. One DNA strand serves as the template strand, which the enzyme reads in the 3′ to 5′ direction while synthesising RNA in the 5′ to 3′ direction. The RNA transcript is complementary to the template strand, with uracil replacing thymine.

Transcription generally consists of initiation, elongation and termination.

During initiation, RNA polymerase and associated transcription factors assemble at the promoter. In bacteria, sigma factors contribute to promoter recognition. In eukaryotes, general transcription factors help recruit RNA polymerase II to protein-coding gene promoters.

During elongation, RNA polymerase moves along the DNA template and incorporates complementary ribonucleotides into the growing RNA molecule. Termination releases the transcript and allows the transcription machinery to disengage from the template.

Eukaryotic cells contain three major nuclear RNA polymerases. RNA polymerase I primarily synthesises precursor ribosomal RNA, RNA polymerase II synthesises pre-mRNA and several non-coding RNAs, while RNA polymerase III produces transfer RNA, 5S ribosomal RNA and other small RNAs.

The primary transcript produced by RNA polymerase II undergoes processing to form mature mRNA. Three major processing events are 5′ capping, splicing and 3′ polyadenylation.

The 5′ cap is a modified guanine nucleotide that protects the transcript and contributes to nuclear export and translation initiation. During splicing, the spliceosome removes introns and joins exons. Alternative splicing allows a single gene to produce different mature RNA transcripts. During 3′ processing, the pre-mRNA is cleaved and a poly(A) tail is added.

Mature mRNA is subsequently exported from the nucleus and translated by ribosomes. RNA processing, transcript stability, nuclear export and alternative splicing all contribute to the regulation of gene expression.
