## 2. Scientific Question

Explain the major stages of protein synthesis, describing the roles of messenger RNA, transfer RNA and ribosomes. Discuss translation initiation, elongation and termination, and explain how newly synthesised polypeptides undergo folding and post-translational modification.

## 3. Illustrative AI-Generated Answer

Protein synthesis is the process by which cells produce proteins using genetic information encoded in messenger RNA (mRNA). It occurs through translation, which takes place on ribosomes.

The major components involved are mRNA, transfer RNA (tRNA), ribosomes and amino acids. Messenger RNA carries genetic information from DNA to the ribosome. Transfer RNA transports specific amino acids and contains an anticodon that recognises a complementary codon on mRNA. Ribosomes provide the site where amino acids are joined to form a polypeptide.

Translation consists of three major stages: initiation, elongation and termination.

During initiation, the ribosome assembles on the mRNA and identifies the start codon, usually AUG. An initiator tRNA carrying methionine recognises the start codon, and the large ribosomal subunit joins the complex.

During elongation, aminoacyl-tRNAs enter the ribosome and recognise codons through complementary anticodon interactions. The ribosome forms peptide bonds between amino acids and moves along the mRNA in the 5′ to 3′ direction. The growing polypeptide chain is extended from its amino-terminal end towards its carboxyl-terminal end.

During termination, a stop codon enters the ribosome. Release factors recognise the stop codon and promote the release of the completed polypeptide.

After translation, the polypeptide folds into its functional three-dimensional structure. Some proteins undergo post-translational modifications, such as phosphorylation, glycosylation, acetylation and proteolytic cleavage. These modifications can influence protein activity, stability, localisation and interactions with other molecules.

In eukaryotes, the small ribosomal subunit generally associates with the 5′ cap-associated initiation machinery and scans the mRNA to identify an appropriate start codon context.

## 6. Corrected and Improved Scientific Answer

Protein synthesis is the biological process through which cells produce polypeptides using the information encoded in messenger RNA. Translation occurs on ribosomes and involves coordinated interactions among mRNA, transfer RNA, ribosomal RNA, ribosomal proteins, amino acids and translation factors.

Messenger RNA contains codons, which are three-nucleotide sequences specifying amino acids or translation termination signals. Transfer RNA molecules carry specific amino acids and contain anticodons that recognise complementary mRNA codons. Ribosomes coordinate codon recognition and catalyse peptide-bond formation.

Translation occurs in three major stages: initiation, elongation and termination.

During initiation, the small ribosomal subunit associates with the mRNA and the initiator tRNA. The start codon, usually AUG, establishes the reading frame. The large ribosomal subunit subsequently joins to form the functional ribosome.

Initiation differs between organisms. In bacteria, the initiator tRNA carries formylmethionine, and ribosome positioning commonly involves the Shine–Dalgarno sequence. In eukaryotes, initiation generally involves recognition of the 5′ cap and scanning by the small ribosomal subunit to locate an appropriate start codon.

During elongation, aminoacyl-tRNAs enter the A site of the ribosome and recognise complementary codons. The growing polypeptide is associated with the P site. The peptidyl transferase centre, formed primarily by ribosomal RNA, catalyses peptide-bond formation. The ribosome then translocates along the mRNA, moving in the 5′ to 3′ direction. Deacylated tRNAs leave through the E site.

The polypeptide chain grows from its amino-terminal end towards its carboxyl-terminal end.

During termination, one of the three stop codons—UAA, UAG or UGA—enters the decoding site. Release factors recognise the stop signal and promote the release of the completed polypeptide.

Following translation, the polypeptide undergoes folding to acquire its functional three-dimensional structure. Molecular chaperones assist the folding of many proteins. Some proteins also undergo post-translational modifications, including phosphorylation, glycosylation, acetylation, methylation and proteolytic cleavage.

These modifications can regulate protein activity, stability, intracellular localisation and interactions. Some proteins are further processed through the endoplasmic reticulum and Golgi apparatus before reaching their final destinations.

Together, translation, protein folding and post-translational modification ensure that cells produce functional proteins capable of carrying out their biological roles.
