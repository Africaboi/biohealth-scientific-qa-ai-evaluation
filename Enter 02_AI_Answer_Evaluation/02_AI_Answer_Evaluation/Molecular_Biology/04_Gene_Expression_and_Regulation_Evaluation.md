## 2. Scientific Question

Explain the major stages of gene expression and discuss how gene expression is regulated in prokaryotic and eukaryotic cells. Include the roles of transcription factors, operons, epigenetic modifications and non-coding RNAs in the regulation of gene activity.

## 3. Illustrative AI-Generated Answer

Gene expression is the process through which information encoded in genes is used to produce functional RNA molecules or proteins. It involves transcription and, for protein-coding genes, translation.

Gene expression is regulated to ensure that cells produce the appropriate gene products at the correct time and in suitable quantities. Regulation can occur at transcriptional, post-transcriptional, translational and post-translational levels.

In prokaryotes, gene regulation commonly occurs through operons. An operon is a group of related genes controlled by shared regulatory sequences. The lac operon in Escherichia coli is an example. It contains genes involved in lactose utilisation and is regulated by the availability of lactose and glucose.

When lactose is absent, the lac repressor binds to the operator and reduces transcription. When lactose is available, allolactose binds to the repressor and reduces its ability to bind the operator. This permits transcription. Low glucose availability also increases cyclic AMP, allowing the cAMP–CAP complex to enhance transcription.

In eukaryotes, gene expression is regulated through transcription factors that bind to promoters, enhancers and other regulatory DNA sequences. Chromatin structure also influences gene accessibility. DNA methylation and histone modifications can alter transcriptional activity.

Non-coding RNAs, including microRNAs, can regulate gene expression after transcription. MicroRNAs commonly bind to target messenger RNAs and promote their degradation or inhibit translation.

Gene expression is therefore controlled by several interacting mechanisms that enable cells to respond to environmental signals, maintain specialised functions and coordinate development.

## 6. Corrected and Improved Scientific Answer

Gene expression is the process through which genetic information is used to produce functional RNA molecules or proteins. Protein-coding genes are generally expressed through transcription followed by translation, while non-protein-coding genes produce functional RNA molecules such as transfer RNA, ribosomal RNA and regulatory non-coding RNAs.

Gene expression is regulated at several levels, including transcriptional, post-transcriptional, translational and post-translational regulation.

In prokaryotes, transcriptional regulation frequently involves operons. An operon consists of multiple related genes controlled by shared regulatory DNA sequences. The lac operon of Escherichia coli is a well-studied example involved in lactose utilisation.

When lactose is absent, the lac repressor binds to the operator and inhibits transcription. When lactose is present, its derivative allolactose binds to the repressor and reduces its affinity for the operator, allowing transcription to occur.

Glucose availability also influences lac operon expression. When glucose levels are low, intracellular cAMP generally increases. cAMP binds to the catabolite activator protein (CAP), and the resulting complex can bind near the lac promoter to enhance transcription.

In eukaryotes, gene expression is regulated through interactions among transcription factors, regulatory DNA sequences, chromatin-remodelling complexes and other proteins. General transcription factors help recruit RNA polymerase II to many protein-coding gene promoters, while specific activators and repressors influence transcription in response to developmental and environmental signals.

Chromatin organisation also plays an important role. DNA methylation, particularly at promoter-associated CpG sites, is often associated with transcriptional repression. Histone acetylation commonly promotes a more accessible chromatin environment, while histone methylation can either activate or repress transcription depending on the modified residue and cellular context.

Post-transcriptional regulation includes RNA processing, alternative splicing, RNA editing in relevant transcripts, nuclear export and control of mRNA stability. MicroRNAs regulate gene expression by associating with Argonaute-containing silencing complexes and recognising target messenger RNAs. This interaction can promote mRNA degradation or inhibit translation.

Translational regulation influences the rate at which mRNA is converted into protein. Post-translational regulation includes protein modification, intracellular targeting and regulated protein degradation.

Together, these mechanisms enable cells to coordinate gene activity, respond to environmental conditions, maintain specialised functions and regulate development.
