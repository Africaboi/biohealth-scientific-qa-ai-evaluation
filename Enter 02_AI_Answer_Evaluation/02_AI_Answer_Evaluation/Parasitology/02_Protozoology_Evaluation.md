## 5. Corrected Scientific Answer

Protozoa are unicellular eukaryotic organisms that include free-living and parasitic forms. Medically important protozoa may inhabit the intestine, blood, tissues, or other body compartments and can cause diseases through different mechanisms.

### A. Plasmodium Species

*Plasmodium* species are apicomplexan parasites responsible for malaria. Important human-infecting species include *P. falciparum*, *P. vivax*, *P. malariae*, *P. ovale* species, and *P. knowlesi*.

**Transmission and life cycle**

Malaria is usually transmitted through the bite of an infected female *Anopheles* mosquito. The mosquito introduces sporozoites into the human host.

The major stages include:

1. **Sporozoite stage:** Sporozoites enter the bloodstream and invade liver cells.
2. **Liver stage:** Parasites multiply within hepatocytes and produce merozoites. Some species, particularly *P. vivax* and *P. ovale*, can form dormant liver stages called hypnozoites.
3. **Blood stage:** Merozoites invade red blood cells and develop through ring, trophozoite, and schizont stages.
4. **Gametocyte formation:** Some blood-stage parasites develop into male and female gametocytes.
5. **Mosquito stage:** A mosquito ingests gametocytes during a blood meal. Sexual reproduction and subsequent development occur in the mosquito, eventually producing sporozoites that can be transmitted to another human.

**Disease mechanisms**

Malaria symptoms are associated particularly with blood-stage infection, red blood cell destruction, and host inflammatory responses. Severe malaria, especially that caused by *P. falciparum*, can involve multiple organs.

### B. Entamoeba histolytica

*Entamoeba histolytica* is an intestinal protozoan parasite responsible for amoebiasis.

**Transmission and life cycle**

Infection usually occurs through ingestion of mature cysts in contaminated food or water.

After ingestion:

- Cysts survive passage through the stomach.
- Excystation releases trophozoites in the intestine.
- Trophozoites multiply in the large intestine.
- Some organisms encyst and are passed in faeces.
- Invasive trophozoites may penetrate the intestinal mucosa.

**Disease mechanisms**

Intestinal invasion may cause inflammation, ulceration, abdominal discomfort, and amoebic dysentery. In some cases, the parasite spreads through the bloodstream to the liver and causes an amoebic liver abscess.

### C. Giardia duodenalis

*Giardia duodenalis*, also known as *G. intestinalis* or *G. lamblia*, is a flagellated intestinal protozoan that causes giardiasis.

**Transmission and life cycle**

Infection occurs through ingestion of cysts, commonly in contaminated water or food.

The cysts release trophozoites in the small intestine. Trophozoites attach to the intestinal epithelial surface using a specialised ventral adhesive disc. They multiply and may subsequently encyst as they pass through the intestine.

**Disease mechanisms**

Giardiasis may interfere with normal intestinal function and nutrient absorption. Clinical manifestations can include diarrhoea, abdominal discomfort, bloating, and malabsorption.

### D. Reproduction in Protozoa

Protozoan reproduction varies among groups.

- **Binary fission:** One organism divides into two daughter cells.
- **Multiple fission:** The nucleus divides repeatedly before the cytoplasm separates into multiple daughter cells.
- **Sexual processes:** Some protozoa undergo sexual reproduction or genetic exchange during particular life-cycle stages.

For example, *Plasmodium* undergoes asexual multiplication in humans and sexual reproduction in mosquitoes.

### E. Diagnostic Importance

The choice of diagnostic method depends on the parasite and clinical presentation.

- Malaria may be diagnosed using blood-film microscopy, rapid diagnostic tests, or molecular methods.
- Amoebiasis may be investigated using stool microscopy, antigen detection, or molecular testing. Microscopy alone may not reliably distinguish *E. histolytica* from morphologically similar species.
- Giardiasis may be diagnosed using stool microscopy, antigen detection, or molecular assays.

Understanding parasite morphology, developmental stages, and transmission is essential for accurate diagnosis and disease control.
